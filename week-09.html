Nobody leaves the building without a line on this board. The line is not the paperwork — the line is the reason anyone knows to go and look.

A return time may be amended by the duty officer and by nobody else, and the amendment is signed. Time out, name and reason are what was written when the person left, and they are not amendable at all. If those are wrong the line is void and a new one is written.

One person, one open line. Nobody has two. An amendment goes on the open line and never on a closed one, because a closed line is a record of something that already finished.

Separate from the watch log, and older than it. Every temperature anybody has read off the masts this season is written down here, one line each: the day, the time, the reading, and the name of whoever went out for it. Nothing is ever taken out of it.

It is a record, not a summary. Nobody has ever added it up. Working out the season's average, or which day was the coldest, or who has done the most MET runs, would mean going through fifty thousand lines by hand — so the questions get asked in the mess and answered by whoever sounds most confident.

Standing orders revised day 255

  • Colder than −50 °C, or a blizzard warning standing: nobody goes out. Not for five minutes, not for a photograph.
  • Sign out. Say where. Say when you are back. Take the radio and turn it on.
  • Vents and exhaust intakes are checked every watch. A buried exhaust does not announce itself — it just fills the building.
  • Comms window to the mainland is 21:00–21:40. Outside that we are on our own, which is the normal condition.
  • The generator is the whole building. Anything odd about the sound of it wakes the mech, day or night.
  • An amended return time is signed and timed. A board nobody can be held to is worse than no board.
  • One person, one open line. Nobody is signed out twice at once, and an amendment goes on the line that is still open. A closed line is a record of something that already happened and is not rewritten. Added day 255, after an audit found both.
  • Everything that happens on a watch goes in the one book, in the order it happened. A sign-out, a reading off the masts and a dip of the day tank are not three different records kept in three different places. They are three lines, and the only thing they have in common is that somebody wrote each one down at a time.
  • A reading is not a reading without a name and a time on it. The met book is kept per entry — day, clock, temperature, and who went out for it. A column of numbers with nobody attached is weather; this is a record of work done by people in the dark.
  • Three trips out in a day and the duty officer sends somebody else. The tally is kept per person, not for the station — a crew of twelve averaging one trip each is an ordinary day, and one person doing all twelve is how people get hurt.

Haldane Station is not a real place. It is the demonstration world for a college .NET course, and everything above is invented — the crew, the log, the weather. The station is named for the Haldanes, physiologists of dangerous air, which is why the thing most likely to kill you here is a blocked exhaust rather than the cold.
